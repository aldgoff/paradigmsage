### The Circular Argument

"This statement is true."

At first glance, this statement is trivial: it must be true,
but a careful analysis reveals that false is also valid.
A statement whose truth claim is false is false,
so if this statement is false, then its claim is false.
Both true and false are valid solutions.
Note the consequences: the solution is multi-valued,
or perhaps indeterminate - pick a value, either one, deliberately, or randomly.

### The Liar's Paradox

"This statement is false."

At first glance, this statement is a contradiction;
if true, it must be false, and if false, it must be true.
G. Spencer-Brown introduced the notion of *imaginary truthvalues*.
Like the Boolean truthvalues, they come in pairs:
imaginary true and imaginary false.
Note the consequences: the solution is multi-valued,
or perhaps indeterminate - pick a value, either one, deliberately, or randomly.

### Complex Math

The model for his solution is the pair of self-referential equations: 

`x = +1/x` 

`x = -1/x`

Note how 'x' refers to itself.
The first is multi-valued in the real numbers (both forms of unity work); 
the second is multi-valued in the imaginary numbers (+i, -i). 
In both the mathematical and logical domains, self-reference can break closure.
Restoring it may require the invention of a new type of value.

A look back reveals the larger pattern.

`x = 1 - x` 

has no solutions in the integers; we must 'invent' the rationals.

`x = 2 / x` 

has no solutions in the rationals; we must 'invent' the irrationals.

`x = -1 / x` 

has no solutions in the reals; we must 'invent' the imaginaries.
