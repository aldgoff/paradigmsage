### Classical Paradigm

In the classical paradigm, causality is straightforward:
if A and B are correlated, then either A causes B, or B causes A,
or some third prior event causes both.

### Quantum Paradigm

Consider two spacelike separated observers, Alice and Bob,
who share a sequence of EPR pairs in the antisymmetric state.
When they measure in the same basis, the outcomes for each pair will be opposite.

Prior to either measurement, the state of the pair is entangled - nonseparable.
Upon either measurement, the pair becomes disentangled, with each particle reduced to an independent eigenstate.
The eigenvalues are still correlated, but what happens to one from now on has no effect on the other; they are separable.
This is referred to as *collapse of the wave function* (in some circles, as the *reduction*).
In modern parlance, this reduction (collapse) occurs *instantaneously*.
This view is problematic, since 'instantaneous' is not a relativistic invariant.

Each observer can tell a story about causality:
- Each claims that they receive a steady stream of particles in an *entangled* state.
- Each claims it is their observation that *caused* the collapse of the wave function, 
reducing the companion particle to an independent eigenstate.
- Each claims that the other observer receives a steady stream of particles in a *mixture* of random states.

The causal stories are not the same – and are not compatible.
The mathematical histories of each particle pair differ between the two stories.
But both stories predict the same observables.
Is it really the case that both stories can be 'true' at the same time?

Call this the *Ambiguous Collapse Paradigm* (ACP).

### Spacelike Causality

The ACP may in fact be the way the world is.
It is jarring because the idea of two incompatible causal stories both being valid is counterintuitive.
An alternative is *spacelike causality*, where one and only one measurement is the *actual cause* of the collapse.
Such an alternative theory would have to specify how the particulars of the 
nonlocal situation *preferentially select* one measurement over the other.
Such a theory could not be tested directly, because both collapses yield the same observable statistics.
There would have to be side effects, or perhaps other quantum behaviors, for which the theory
could make testable predictions about experimental outcomes.
